Most AEO advice focuses on blog articles, but your service pages are where buying decisions happen. If those pages are vague, buried in jargon or short on specifics, they are hard to quote and hard to trust.

## Open with a clear definition

Begin each service page with a plain statement of what the service is, who it is for and what outcome it produces, in two or three sentences. For example: 'Local SEO helps businesses that serve a specific area appear in map results and local searches. It suits clinics, law firms and home services that rely on nearby customers.'

## Answer the buyer's questions on the page

Add sections that cover the questions people ask before hiring.

- What is included, and what is not?
- How does the process work, step by step?
- How long does it take to see results?
- What does it cost, or what drives the cost?
- Who is it right for, and who is it not?
- What proof do you have, such as case studies and reviews?

## Be specific

Replace 'tailored solutions' and 'holistic strategy' with concrete details. Name the deliverables, timelines, tools and outcomes. Specific content is more likely to be extracted and more believable to humans.

## Add structure and signals

Use clear headings, a short FAQ, Service and Organization structured data, visible reviews and links to relevant articles and case studies. Make the next step obvious with a clear call to action.

Treat each service page as the best possible answer to the question 'Should I hire someone for this, and why you?' When that question is answered clearly, the page serves both machines and buyers.

Google released its third core update of the year on November 10. If you run a site, manage SEO for clients, or report organic numbers to leadership, someone has probably already asked you about it. One person saw a dip. Someone else saw a spike. Everyone wants to know what changed.

My view is simple. Right now, nobody outside Google knows exactly what changed. But we know enough to plan the next three weeks well. This piece covers what Google has confirmed, what I’m telling my clients, and how to read your data without fooling yourself.

### What Google has confirmed

The facts are short.

The update went live on the Google Search Status Dashboard at 10:06 a.m. Pacific time on November 10, 2026. For those of us in India, that was close to midnight. Google says it applies globally, across all languages, and the rollout may take up to two weeks.

Google’s Search Central team also posted on LinkedIn, calling it a regular update meant to “better surface relevant, satisfying content for searchers from all types of sites.” Search Engine Journal noted this is the same line Google used for the March core update.

That’s about it. No Search Central blog post. No stated goals. No hint about which sites or queries are in focus. Google’s guidance page on core updates hasn’t been revised since December 2025.

So if you see a post this week claiming to know exactly what the November update targets, be careful. It’s a guess dressed up as insight.

### Where this update sits in 2026

Context helps. This is the eighth ranking update on the status dashboard this year:

- **March core update:** March 27 to April 8 (about 12 days)
- **May core update:** May 21 to June 2 (about 12 days)
- **June spam update:** about 2 days
- **August spam update:** about 3 days
- **September spam update:** September 24 to October 8 (close to 14 days)
- **November core update:** started November 10

Two things stand out. First, more than five months passed between the end of the May core update and this one. That’s a long gap, and longer gaps often mean more change lands at once. Second, both earlier core updates this year took around 12 days. If this one follows the pattern, expect it to finish somewhere between November 22 and 24.

### What to do while it rolls out

My advice to clients this week is mostly about what not to do.

**Don’t make big changes mid-rollout.** Rankings will move up and down for the next two weeks. Some of that is noise. If you rewrite 40 pages or prune half your blog on day four, you’ll never know whether the final result came from Google or from you.

**Don’t react to daily rank tracker swings.** Rank trackers are useful, but during a core update they show you a moving picture one frame at a time. Watch the trend, not the day.

**Do keep shipping your normal work.** If you had content or technical fixes planned before November 10, carry on. A core update is not a reason to freeze. It’s a reason to avoid panic edits.

**Do write down what you see.** Keep a simple log: date, what moved, which pages, which queries. When the rollout ends, this log will save you hours.

**Do brief your stakeholders now.** If you report to a CMO or a founder, tell them today that some movement is expected and that you’ll share a proper read after the rollout. Setting expectations before a drop is far easier than explaining one after.

### How to read the data afterwards

Google’s guidance on timing is clear. Wait at least one full week after the rollout is complete. Then compare that week in Search Console against a week before the update began. For this update, that baseline is the week before November 10.

Simple on paper. This time, the calendar makes it tricky.

**For Indian sites, the baseline week is festive.** Diwali fell on November 8 this year, with the festival running roughly from November 6 to 10. So the “clean” week before the update, November 3 to 9, carries festive traffic for most ecommerce, travel, gifting, finance, and local service businesses in India. Comparing a post-Diwali week against a Diwali week will make many sites look worse than they really are.

**For sites with US traffic, the comparison week is a holiday.** If the rollout runs the full two weeks and ends around November 24, the first week after it includes Thanksgiving, Black Friday, and Cyber Monday. Retail sites would be comparing holiday traffic against a normal week, which will make things look better than they are.

For many of my clients, the default comparison simply won’t work. Here’s how I’m handling it:

1. **Use year-over-year as a sanity check.** Compare the post-update period with the same festive window last year. Diwali was on October 20 in 2025, so line up the days around the festival, not the calendar dates.
2. **Pick a quieter baseline.** A late-October week, before festive buying peaked, may be a cleaner benchmark than the week Google suggests.
3. **Watch average position and impressions, not just clicks.** Clicks swing with demand. Position tells you whether Google’s view of your page has actually changed.
4. **Segment before you conclude.** Break the data down by page type, query intent, and country. A sitewide drop is rare. Usually a group of pages or a type of query moves, and that tells you where to look.
5. **Check your competitors.** If everyone in your niche dropped, it may be a demand shift or a change in how the results page looks. If only you dropped, it’s more likely about your content.

### If you were hit, what then

Google has said for years that core updates don’t target specific sites or pages. They reassess how well content meets people’s needs across the web. That framing matters. A drop is rarely a penalty. Nothing is broken on your site in a technical sense. Google has simply decided other pages answer the query better.

So recovery isn’t about one fix. When I audit a site after a core update, I work through a plain set of questions:

- **Does the page answer the query better than what now ranks above it?** Open the top three results and be honest. The question isn’t “is our page good.” It’s “is our page the best one here.”
- **Is there real experience behind the content?** Pages written by someone who has done the work, with original data, examples, or screenshots, hold up better than summaries of other summaries.
- **Is the page built for the reader or for the keyword?** Long intros, repeated headings, and filler FAQs written for search engines are easy for people to spot. Google is getting better at spotting them too.
- **Is the site focused?** Sites that drift far from their core topic to chase traffic often see those side sections slip first.
- **Is the experience getting in the way?** Heavy ads, pushy popups, and slow mobile pages make even good content feel worse.

One more thing, said plainly. Recovery often takes time. Improvements you make now may not fully show until a later core update. That’s frustrating, but it’s also why quick tactics rarely work here.

### If you gained, don’t relax

Gains deserve the same scrutiny as losses. Find out which pages rose and why. Was it fresher content? Better internal linking? A competitor that slipped? The answer tells you what to do more of, and how stable that gain is likely to be.

### My take

Core updates are a routine part of the job now. Three this year, with spam updates in between. The teams that handle them well don’t have a secret playbook. They have clean data, honest content reviews, and the patience to wait before drawing conclusions.

This one comes with a twist. The festive calendar in India and the holiday calendar in the US both make Google’s standard comparison less reliable than usual. Plan your measurement around that now, while the update is still rolling out, and you’ll make better calls in December.
